# Macro interview cards: shortage → normalization → dealer pressure

19 October 2026. Concise synthesis of existing research, not a new empirical study. Mechanisms below are distinct from causal estimates; we have not allocated lost sales precisely among them.

## The 30-second answer

“The pandemic disrupted vehicle production, especially through semiconductor shortages. Scarce vehicles supported unusually strong pricing and dealer margins. As supply recovered, customers regained alternatives, but high vehicle prices and expensive financing still constrained affordability. Stellantis entered that more competitive market with an uneven product offer and too much inventory in some configurations. Dealers needed more discounts to sell those vehicles and became less willing to replenish them. That converted a customer-sales problem into a larger factory-shipment and earnings problem.”

## 1. Before the shortage: a competitive reference point

- **2019 is a useful comparison, not a perfect equilibrium or guaranteed recovery target.** It predates pandemic scarcity, but brands already differed in demand, fleet exposure, incentives and product age.
- Incentives and dealer inventory existed before COVID. Their return is not itself evidence of management failure.
- Compare like-for-like products where possible: later three-row variants, powertrains and discontinued models change what a nameplate total represents.

## 2. Pandemic and chip shortage: scarcity changed the economics

- **A missing low-cost chip can prevent delivery of a much more expensive vehicle.** Complete, sellable vehicles matter; the missing component's share of the bill of materials does not measure its production impact.
- Semiconductor constraints were part of broader factory, supplier and logistics disruption. Do not assign every later launch problem to the pandemic chip shortage.
- With fewer competing vehicles on lots, buyers had fewer substitutes. OEMs could reduce incentives; dealers could retain more gross profit per vehicle.
- **Fewer units could coexist with strong profits.** Higher net realization, richer mix and cost actions could offset lower volume. That does not prove household demand became structurally stronger.

## 3. Normalization: supply returned faster than pricing conditions normalized

- **Supply recovered progressively—not through a single date when chips suddenly “came back.”** More production and deliveries replenished empty lots and restored competitors' availability.
- More inventory initially represents recovery from scarcity, not necessarily overproduction. It becomes excessive when receipts persistently exceed customer sell-through at the offered price.
- **Availability and affordability are different.** A car can be in stock while its price, borrowing cost, trade-in position and running costs make it unattractive to the buyer.
- Buyers could compare alternatives again. Scarcity-era margins and low incentives became harder to sustain.
- “Normalization” therefore means a return of competition and discounting; it does not require every OEM to lose volume or the whole industry to have weak demand.

## 4. Why dealers became frustrated

- **The dealer owns inventory and typically finances it.** A slow-selling vehicle ties up borrowing capacity and incurs floorplan interest; rising rates make slow turns more painful.
- To move an aged vehicle, the dealer may surrender its own margin, obtain OEM support, or combine both. A big advertised discount does not tell us who paid for it.
- **High stock can coexist with shortages of desirable configurations.** Too many expensive or unwanted versions do not solve a shortage of the engine, trim or payment point shoppers actually want.
- Support that is limited, conditional or complicated can leave dealers uncertain about the margin they will ultimately earn. A coupon is financial support subject to program rules, not necessarily a free vehicle or an automatic invoice reduction.
- Sales-linked bonuses can reward sell-through, but ambitious thresholds can still distort behavior. Such programs are not a wholly new invention, nor have we established a universal switch across all Stellantis brands.

## 5. Why Stellantis suffered more: the company-specific layer

- **Pricing and product choices:** expensive configurations, lost entry offerings and model gaps made the product basket harder to sell as competition returned. This varied by model; it was not a uniform price increase on every vehicle.
- **Execution:** Ram's MY2025 changeover and missing desired configurations constrained the available offer. Other models had different problems; Grand Cherokee's powertrain mix changed sharply during 2024.
- **Dealer economics:** inventory clearance and weak new-vehicle gross profit strained relationships and the economics of fresh orders. Asbury's later improvement provides evidence of repair at one operator, not proof of national normalization.
- **What remains unproven:** a complete competitor-matched effective wholesale-price series; the share of lost sales caused by each mechanism; a nationwide end to stock-conditioned support.

## 6. How inventory amplified the income-statement decline

**Simplified identity for matched geography and vehicle scope:**

`Factory deliveries into the channel ≈ customer sales + change in channel inventory`

- During inventory accumulation, factory shipments can exceed final customer sales, supporting reported OEM revenue temporarily.
- During destocking, dealers sell some vehicles already bought from the OEM and order fewer replacements. Factory shipments can therefore fall faster than customer sales.
- OEM revenue is generally recognized when control transfers under the applicable sales terms, not when a household eventually buys the car. Shipment declines, weaker net pricing and reduced factory fixed-cost absorption can then hit earnings together.
- **Hypothetical illustration:** customer sales fall from 100 to 90, while inventory moves from a 10-unit build to a 10-unit reduction. Shipments fall from 110 to 80—27%, versus a 10% customer-sales decline.
- Actual reported North American shipments and US customer sales do not share the same scope. Do not subtract them to manufacture an exact inventory roll-forward.

## 7. What would make recovery sustainable?

- **Healthy replenishment, not just clearance:** customers buy newly delivered vehicles and dealers reorder them at acceptable margins after incentives and carrying costs.
- Competitive payments and desired configurations improve conversion without ever-larger discounts.
- Inventory settles at a workable level relative to sales; pandemic-low stock is not the goal.
- Dealer comments become more positive **and** operational evidence improves: fresh-stock turns, actual reorders and earned gross profit.
- A shipment rebound from ending destocking is finite. Longer-term volume growth needs customer demand, product coverage or share gains.
