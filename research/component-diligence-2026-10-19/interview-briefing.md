# Five component-cost mechanisms to explain in the interview

## 1. Buy the same part more cheaply

A truck needs four tires whether or not Stellantis negotiates well. Purchasing can lower their invoice price without changing the truck. But the supplier must fund that reduction through better productivity, more volume, less margin or another commercial concession. We should ask whether the reduction is signed, incremental to ordinary annual productivity and net of inflation or compensation elsewhere.

**Interview answer:** “I distinguish a cheaper invoice from cheaper engineering. Both can improve Stellantis's cost, but an unsupported supplier-margin squeeze is less durable than removing real waste.”

## 2. Deliver the same function with fewer components

A smart electrical center can replace some conventional boxes, relays and wiring. Aptiv's demonstration reports $40 per vehicle, including the affected wiring. This is tangible but small relative to our roughly €2,140 per reference vehicle component-savings requirement. It needs equivalent starting hardware, validation and a supplier price change before it can be booked for Stellantis.

**Interview answer:** “I look for old hardware removed, replacement hardware added, net unit saving and vehicles receiving the change. Fewer parts alone is not a cost estimate.”

## 3. Share hidden components without making every brand identical

Different seat upholstery can sit on a common underlying structure. Common controllers, brackets or mechanisms can spread engineering and tooling over more units and simplify purchasing. The constraint is whether load, packaging, comfort and safety requirements really match. A heavy-duty pickup and compact SUV cannot simply share every component.

**Interview answer:** “Commonality works best where customers do not value the difference, and engineers can preserve the required performance. I would not apply a development-resource saving directly to the recurring parts bill.”

## 4. Choose an architecture that is cheaper as a complete system

A smaller turbo engine can require expensive boosting, injection and cooling. A cheaper battery chemistry can require a heavier, larger pack. A range-extender reduces battery requirements relative to some BEVs but adds an engine, generator, fuel, emissions and thermal hardware. Compare complete systems at the same performance and packaging requirements.

**Interview answer:** “Cheaper material or fewer cylinders does not necessarily mean a cheaper equivalent vehicle. The replacement system and integration costs determine the result.”

## 5. Convert an engineering saving into earnings before 2028

A validated design must reach tooling, supplier production and vehicle sales. A late-2028 start does not deliver a full year of savings. Upfront tooling is cash investment, not necessarily immediate AOI expense; engineering and depreciation require their own accounting treatment. Savings on parts still in inventory have not automatically reached earnings.

**Interview answer:** “I separate terminal unit economics, implementation economics and in-year earnings. The best technical idea can contribute little to our forecast horizon if it reaches too few vehicles too late.”

## Questions that would change the investment conclusion

1. Of the reviewed component spend, how much has approved net-price reductions with a production date?
2. What is the old and new L2+ system cost, and how many already-equipped vehicles receive it in each year?
3. How much of gross VCP is ordinary purchasing productivity already in the baseline?
4. What tooling, engineering, supplier compensation or additional feature content offsets the savings?
5. What is the split between current-product changes and new-platform changes? What fraction reaches sold vehicles by January versus December 2028?

## Our current position

The 9% cumulative component reduction is an ambitious historical-benchmark scenario. Public technical evidence makes the mechanisms credible, but has not validated the entire €3.15bn regional benefit. An incomplete public project list cannot prove management is wrong; it does identify the evidence needed to underwrite the forecast.
